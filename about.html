How this works — all of it

Most personality products treat their items, scoring, and norms as trade secrets. This page is the opposite commitment: everything the quiz does is described here, and the entire implementation is readable source code. The design rationale — including which documented criticisms of personality testing each choice answers — is in the project's design notes.

What the quiz measures

The five-factor ("Big Five") model of personality: Negative Emotionality (often called Neuroticism), Extraversion, Openness, Agreeableness, and Conscientiousness, each measured through six narrower facets (30 facets total). The five-factor structure is the closest thing personality science has to a consensus taxonomy: it emerged repeatedly from independent research programs and replicates across dozens of languages. Facet-level detail is reported because facets routinely predict outcomes better than their parent domains — and because two people with identical domain scores can differ meaningfully underneath.

The items

The instrument is the IPIP-NEO-120: the 120-item inventory assembled by John A. Johnson (2014) from the International Personality Item Pool. IPIP items are public domain — one of the very few validated item families that can be freely embedded — and every item this quiz shows is in data/items.js, exactly as administered, with its facet assignment and scoring direction.

  • 120 statements, 4 per facet, answered on a 5-point accuracy scale ("Very inaccurate" … "Very accurate").
  • 65 items are scored positively; 55 are reverse-keyed (disagreement raises the score). The mix counters acquiescence bias — the tendency to drift toward "agree".
  • Item order is Johnson's original administration order (items cycle through the 30 facets), kept for comparability with his norms. Items are not randomized.
  • Two instructed-response reading checks ("answer Moderately inaccurate here") are interleaved at fixed positions. They are never scored; they only inform the low-engagement notice.
  • Provenance: item texts, facet map, and keying were cross-checked between two independent packagings of Johnson's instrument (Alheimsins b5-johnson-120 v6.0.13 and five-factor-e v1.13.1, retrieved 2026-08-30); they agreed on 120/120 facet assignments and keyings and 119/120 texts verbatim (item 58 differs by one conjunction between packagings; the IPIP original wording is used).

Scoring, in full

Worked example. The Anxiety facet (N1) has four items. Suppose you answer 4, 2, 5, 3 (where 1–5 runs from "Very inaccurate" to "Very accurate"):

  1. Reverse-keyed items are flipped: scored = 6 − response. If the second item were reverse-keyed, your 2 becomes a 4.
  2. Facet raw score = sum of its 4 scored items → here 4+4+5+3 = 16 (possible range 4–20). Domain raw = sum of its 6 facet raws (range 24–120).
  3. Standardize against the norm table: z = (raw − mean) ÷ sd. If the N1 norm is mean 12.26, sd 3.72, then z = (16 − 12.26) ÷ 3.72 ≈ 1.01.
  4. Percentile = Φ(z), the standard normal cumulative distribution → Φ(1.01) ≈ 84th percentile.

Every scale on your results page is computed exactly this way — see js/scoring.js.

Percentiles therefore assume scale scores are approximately normally distributed in the norm sample. That is an approximation (bounded sums are never perfectly normal, especially near the extremes) — treat 99th/1st percentile results as "far out on this dimension," not as precise one-in-a-hundred claims.

The norms

Raw scores become percentiles by comparison with Johnson's IPIP-NEO norm tables, as distributed with his scoring program: the ages 21–40 cohort, male and female tables averaged. Johnson's norm data come from a very large internet sample (the 2014 paper reports N = 619,150 for the 120-item form). The exact means and SDs used are in data/norms.js. Honest caveats:

  • It is an internet convenience sample, not a census-representative one.
  • One combined table is used for everyone. Average scores differ somewhat by sex and age (e.g., Negative Emotionality declines with age), so if you are far from the 21–40 combined reference group, your percentiles are correspondingly approximate.
  • No demographic data is collected — the accuracy cost of one shared table was chosen over the privacy and friction cost of asking.

Confidence bands

Every score is shown with a shaded band: the standard error of measurement, computed as SEM = sd × √(1 − α) where α is scale reliability. Reliabilities are assumed working values (facet α = 0.75, domain α = 0.88, in line with typical published values for this inventory — Johnson 2014 reports mean facet α ≈ 0.76), not values re-estimated on this site's users; they are disclosed in data/norms.js. Facet bands are wide because four items is a short scale — that width is honest. Scores inside your band are statistically indistinguishable from yours.

The archetype rules, exactly

The archetype is a summary of your most distinctive dimensions, never a replacement for the scores. The assignment rule (js/archetype.js):

  • Rank your five domain z-scores by absolute value.
  • Two or more at |z| ≥ 0.4 (roughly beyond the 34th–66th percentile band): the top two, with their directions, name a pair archetype — 40 exist.
  • Exactly one: a single-pole archetype — 10 exist.
  • None: the balanced archetype. 51 archetypes total.

This deliberately avoids the classic type-test design of splitting all five dimensions at their midpoints — which forces near-average scores to pick a side and is the main documented reason type labels flip on retest. Your results page always shows how close you sit to neighboring archetypes and which score would tip you there; gaps are expressed in approximate percentile points (a local conversion near the threshold). If a retake lands you on a neighbor, that is the labeling layer working as designed — compare the continuous scores, which move far less.

Retest honesty

Trait scale scores are quite stable over weeks-to-months (published retest correlations for inventories like this are typically around .8, higher for domains than facets) — but "quite stable" is not "fixed": mood, recent events, and measurement error move individual sittings, and average trait levels genuinely drift across adulthood (conscientiousness and agreeableness tend to rise; negative emotionality tends to fall). This quiz treats that as a feature to measure rather than hide: results are dated, past results are kept on your device, and the compare view shows the actual drift dimension by dimension. No claim is made that your result is a permanent identity, because that claim would be false.

Quality flags

Three low-engagement signals are computed, disclosed here in full: missing either instructed reading check; long runs of identical answers (15+ in a row, or near-zero response variance); and finishing in under 6 minutes. Flags never block or discard a result — they add a visible notice suggesting the scores be read skeptically and the quiz retaken unhurried.

Privacy

Everything runs in your browser. Answers and results are never transmitted anywhere; there is no server, no analytics, and no account. Results persist only in this browser's local storage, and in share links — a share link encodes your 30 facet scores, the date, and the quality flags (35 characters; format documented in js/encode.js) and reveals them to anyone you give it to, so share deliberately.

What this quiz must not be used for

  • Not for hiring, admissions, or any decision about another person. Self-report measures are fakeable under stakes, and the evidence for personality-based selection without job analysis and local validation is weak. (The project's criticisms review covers this in detail, including the legal exposure employers have incurred.)
  • Not a clinical instrument. It measures ordinary trait variation, not disorders; no item probes medical or psychiatric territory. The Negative Emotionality scales describe normal-range emotional style — they cannot detect, rule out, or diagnose anything. If your mood or anxiety is persistently heavy, a professional is the right tool, not a quiz.
  • Not a verdict. Scores describe current tendencies relative to other adults; they are not capacities, destinies, or excuses.

What "accuracy" claims you will not see here

You will not see "97% of users say their results are accurate" — felt accuracy is the one kind of evidence a personality result can earn while being meaningless, since generic flattering text reliably feels accurate (the Barnum effect). The honest basis for this quiz is: public-domain items with published psychometrics, disclosed norms, disclosed math, and result prose written to be specific enough that the opposite profile would reject it. Where the numbers are approximate, the page saying so is this one.
